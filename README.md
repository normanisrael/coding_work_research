# PhD One-Pager

## Computational Design of Photonic Metamaterials: From Subwavelength Gratings to Non-Hermitian Topology
Norman S. Israel 

### Research Overview
My PhD thesis focused on developing new ways to efficiently design and gain physical insight into  photonic metamaterials. Photonic metamaterials are artificially engineered structures that manipulate light in ways not readily achievable with conventional materials. They have promising applications in telecommunications, computing and sensing. Designing them is important, but electromagnetic simulations, though they allow for the modeling of realistic structures, are computationally expensive, particularly when optimizing multiple designs. Meanwhile, simplified theoretical models, though they can allow for rapid exploration, often fail to capture the complexity of realistic structures due to idealistic assumptions.

My PhD research addresses this challenge by finding a balance between the two worlds. The research focuses on two complementary areas: silicon photonic subwavelength gratings and exotic states of light in plasmonic metamaterials.
Key Research Contributions

### 1. Data-Driven Effective Medium Approximation for Silicon Photonic Metamaterials
I developed a data-driven effective-medium approximation for complex subwavelength gratings used in integrated photonic devices. By training deep neural networks on time-domain simulation data, I constructed surrogate models that predict the effective refractive indices of three-dimensional structures. These predictions allow computationally demanding 3D structures to be represented using simplified 2D models.

#### Key achievement: 
Demonstrated that the concept of effective-medium, traditionally limited to infinite 3D structures, can be extended to finite 3D grating structures across telecommunications wavelengths, enabling more efficient exploration of photonic device designs.

This work was conducted in collaboration with researchers at the National Research Council of Canada.

### 2. Exotic States of Light in Plasmonic Metamaterials
I initiated and developed a research direction investigating exotic states of light known as topological states in periodic arrays of metallic nanoresonators. 

These structures naturally exhibit optical losses, making them attractive platforms for studying non-Hermitian physics. In these systems, losses, which are traditionally seen as problematic, could be leveraged as an advantage for technological applications such as telecommunications, computing and sensing. Lacking, however, is a theoretical formalism and computational tools that allows one to design and understand a wide variety of realistic structures that exhibit these exotic states so as to develop them for technological applications. Building on quasinormal-mode coupled-mode theory, I co-developed such a theoretical model as well as developed and applied computational tools to address this gap. I designed and performed analysis of plasmonic nanoresonator chains with spherical and cuboid geometries.

The research investigated how resonator geometry, long-range electromagnetic interactions, and modal composition influence the emergence of localized edge states and their relationship to bulk topological properties.

#### Key achievement: 
Demonstrated and characterized edge-localized electromagnetic modes in plasmonic nanoresonator chains, while examining the predictive capabilities and limitations of the models through comparisons with full lattice electromagnetic simulations.

This research led to an international collaboration with the Max Planck Institute for the Science of Light in Germany.

### Overall Scientific Contribution
Although these two research areas involve different physical systems, they address the same fundamental question: How can we develop computationally efficient models of complex photonic metamaterials without losing the essential physics needed to understand and design them? My thesis demonstrates two complementary strategies: learning effective optical properties from simulation data and constructing physically interpretable models from the resonant modes of individual resonators.
Together, these approaches contribute to the development of more efficient computational tools for photonic metamaterial research and device design.
